Many Incidents

My parents took my birth details to an astrologer and came home with my name, Dharaa: thread, in Kannada, with a second A for numerology. Vastu, India's building science, picked the direction I sleep facing. It was just there, like the plumbing.

I don't believe things until I've checked them. That includes my chart, which I still read for luck: I check its math in Python against the Swiss Ephemeris. But charts taught me one habit: read a life across years, never by a single day.

In July, when Doha is too hot for anything but sitting indoors asking questions, I turned my checking on the Indian migrants I grew up among, my parents' friends and neighbours. I was everyone's daughter and had never asked them anything. So I began an archive of their memories. AI is starting to retell lives like theirs, and I wanted to know how far to trust it. My method: I reduce each memory to checkable facts, an AI retells it in the person's voice, and I look for the seam: the line where the machine runs out of facts and starts inventing.

Subramanya, nineteen years in Qatar, told me about his daughter. She was one when he came here to find work, and his sister promised to look after her in India. When I read my summary back to him, I had called his sister's promise the one time someone carried him through. "Many times," he said gently. I crossed out "the one time" and wrote "many incidents" above it. Next I asked him for another single moment. He had just told me his life wasn't made of single moments, and I, who read charts across years, hadn't heard him.

He was so kind about it that I almost missed my mistake. That night my English-only transcription app turned his Kannada into noise, so I retyped it myself, three hours at the speed he spoke, Kannada above English, so the words stayed his. "Of our two eyes," he had said, "one is India, one is Qatar."

Across my interviews, I counted the names and phrases from the recordings that the AI's versions dropped: thirty-two. Twenty-four were already missing from my own summaries. The machine had not invented my mistake. It had copied it, then filled the gaps: told only that Subramanya's older son was born in India, it chose a city he is not from and gave his wife a line she never said.

So I changed how I listen. With Sujith, I asked one question and let it run. For half an hour he talked about the family he supports, the people who helped him leave, and the year he was laid off. Nobody in India knows; they picture a lavish life, and he lets them. My old questions would have found none of it. As a summary, he said, his years here would read as a line. I would have written that line. Now every interview ends with one question: what would a summary miss? And everyone reads their transcript first, free to strike any line.

Checking used to mean stripping a story down to what I could prove. Now it also means checking my version against the person, and assuming my first summary has left someone out. Subramanya could correct me because he was in the room. Most people an AI writes about are not. So in my archive, every AI version sits beside the person's recorded voice, gaps showing. Next, each person will mark the seam in their own story: the line where the machine's version stops being their life.

I still sleep facing the direction Vastu chose. But I had also summarized my own name in one word: thread. My father says Dharaa means flowing water, too, in Sanskrit. Naturally, I checked; he's right. Even my name held more than I had written down.
