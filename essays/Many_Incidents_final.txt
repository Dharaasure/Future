Many Incidents

My parents took my birth details to an astrologer and came home with my name, Dharaa: thread, in Kannada, with a second A for numerology. Vastu, India's building science, picked the direction I sleep facing. Nobody asked me to believe any of it. It was just there, like the plumbing.

I believe nothing until I've checked it, which is awkward for someone who still reads her chart for luck. So I check its math in Python against the Swiss Ephemeris. Whether the planets mean anything, I haven't settled. But charts taught me one rule I trust: read a chart whole, across years. Ask it about a single day, and it answers confidently and wrong. I didn't expect that rule to apply to people.

In July, I turned my checking on the Indian migrants I grew up among in Doha, my parents' friends and neighbours. I had been one of their daughters for years and had never asked them anything. So I began an archive of their memories. I reduce each memory to checkable facts, ask an AI to retell it in the person's voice, and look for the seam: the line where the machine runs out of facts and starts inventing.

Subramanya, nineteen years in Qatar, told me about the day his daughter was born. My summary turned his daughter's birth into a single day: the day Qatar proved it cared for him. "Many times," he said gently. Not once: many. I crossed out "the day" and wrote "many incidents" above it. Then I asked about another single day anyway. He had just told me his life wasn't made of single days, and I hadn't heard him.

Being wrong usually embarrasses me. This was quieter and worse: he was so kind about it that I missed it. That night my English-only transcriber turned his Kannada into noise, so I retyped it myself, three hours at the speed he spoke, Kannada above English, so the words stayed his. Typed out, they were lovely. "Of our two eyes," he had said, "one is India, one is Qatar."

The machine did what I had done, only louder. Told that his older son was born in India, it placed the birth in a city he is not from and gave his wife a line she never said. Then I counted what the AI's versions left out: thirty-two names and phrases. Twenty-four of those were already missing from my own summaries. I had built the project to catch a machine, and it caught me first.

So I changed how I listen. With Sujith, I asked one question and let it run. Half an hour in, he told me about the year he was laid off. Nobody in India knows; they picture a lavish life, and he lets them. As a summary, he said, his years here would read as a line. I would have written that line. Now every interview ends with one question: what would a summary miss? And everyone reads their transcript first, free to strike any line.

Subramanya could correct me because he was in the room. Most people a system writes about never are, and a smooth retelling never says where the facts ran out. So in my archive, every AI version sits beside the person's recorded voice, gaps showing. Next, each person will mark the seam in their own story: the line where the machine's version stops being their life.

I still sleep facing the direction I was given. My father says Dharaa also means flowing water, in Sanskrit. Naturally, I checked; he's right. A thread and a river, like a chart, like Subramanya's nineteen years: things you can only read whole.
