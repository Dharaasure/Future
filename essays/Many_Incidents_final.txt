Many Incidents

My parents took my birth details to an astrologer and came home with my name: Dharaa, which means thread in Kannada, with a second A for numerology. Astrology was simply there, like the plumbing. I still read my chart for luck, but I believe nothing I haven't checked, so I recompute its positions in Python against the Swiss Ephemeris. Charts taught me one rule: read a life across years, never by a single day.

In July, too hot to go outside, I turned my checking on people. In Doha, I grew up as everyone's daughter among my parents' Indian friends, and I had never asked them anything. Soon AI would be retelling lives like theirs, so I began recording them, and testing the AI. First, I summarized each memory into checkable facts and read the summary back to the person. Then an AI rewrote my summary in their voice, and I looked for the seam: the first line where the AI made something up.

When Subramanya came to Qatar nineteen years ago, his daughter was one, and his sister promised to look after her in India. My summary called that promise the one time someone carried him through. He corrected me gently: "Many times. Many incidents." It hit me like a jolt. Those two words held years of hospital visits, of kindness from Qatari strangers, of never once feeling abandoned abroad. I crossed out "the one time" and wrote "many incidents" above it. With my very next question, I asked him for another single moment. He had just told me his life was not made of single moments, and I, who read charts across years, had not heard him.

He was so kind about it that I almost missed my mistake. That night, my English-only app turned his Kannada into noise. Unwilling to let another tool shorten him, I retyped the recording myself, three hours at the speed he spoke, Kannada above English, so the words stayed his. At that speed, I heard a line my summary had flattened: "Of our two eyes, one is India, one is Qatar."

Then I counted. Across fifteen retellings, the AI left out thirty-two names and phrases that people had said on my recordings. In my own summaries, twenty-four of those thirty-two were already gone, before the AI ever saw them. The machine had not invented my mistake. It had copied it, then filled the gaps itself, moving his older son's birth to Mangalore, a city he is not from.

So I changed how I listen. With Sujith, I asked one question and let him talk. He talked for half an hour, including the year he was laid off, which nobody in India knows. "To others, my life is just a line," he said. "Only I know the struggles, the relationships, the friendships. They'll just say, 'Wow, good, well done.'" A month earlier, my summary would have reduced him to exactly that line. Now every interview ends with one question: what would a summary of your life miss? And everyone reads their transcript and the AI's version first, free to strike any line. Val said the machine's version of her story had "added a lot of masala."

Checking used to mean stripping a story to what I could prove. Now it also means checking my version against the person and assuming my first summary has left someone out. Subramanya could correct me because he was in the room. Most people an AI writes about will never be. So in my archive, every AI retelling sits beside the person's recorded voice, gaps showing. Next, each person will read the two versions side by side and mark the seam themselves.

I had summarized my own name in one word, too. My father says Dharaa also means flowing water in Sanskrit. I checked; he's right. Even my name held more than I had written down.
