Many Incidents

My parents took my birth details to an astrologer and came home with my name, Dharaa: thread, in Kannada, with a second A for numerology. Vastu, India's building science, picked the direction I sleep facing. It was all just there, like the plumbing.

I don't believe things until I've checked them, which is awkward for someone who still reads her chart for luck, so I check its math in Python against the Swiss Ephemeris. Whether the planets mean anything, I haven't settled. What charts did teach me is to read a life across years, not by a single day.

In July, when Doha is too hot for anything but sitting indoors asking questions, I turned my checking on the Indian migrants I grew up among, my parents' friends and neighbours. I was one of their daughters and had never asked them anything. So I began an archive of their memories. I reduce each memory to checkable facts, ask an AI to retell it in the person's voice, and look for the seam: the line where the machine runs out of facts and starts inventing.

Subramanya, nineteen years in Qatar, told me about his daughter. She was one when he came here to find work, and his sister promised to look after her in India. In my summary, that became the one time someone carried him through. "Many times," he said gently. I crossed out "the one time" and wrote "many incidents" above it. Then I asked him about another single moment anyway. He had just told me his life wasn't made of single moments, and I hadn't heard him.

He was so kind about it that I almost missed my mistake. That night my English-only transcriber turned his Kannada into noise, so I retyped it myself, three hours at the speed he spoke, Kannada above English, so the words stayed his. Typed out, they were lovely. "Of our two eyes," he had said, "one is India, one is Qatar."

The AI made my mistake bigger. Told that his older son was born in India, it placed the birth in a city he is not from and gave his wife a line she never said. Then I counted what the AI's versions left out: thirty-two names and phrases. Twenty-four of those were already missing from my own summaries.

So I changed how I listen. With Sujith, I asked one question and let it run. For half an hour he talked about the family he supports, the people who helped him leave, the year he was laid off, and how it felt to keep that year to himself. Nobody in India knows; they picture a lavish life, and he lets them. As a summary, he said, his years here would read as a line. I would have written that line. Now every interview ends with one question: what would a summary miss? And everyone reads their transcript first, free to strike any line.

Subramanya could correct me because he was in the room. Most people an AI writes about are not. So in my archive, every AI version sits beside the person's recorded voice, gaps showing. Next, each person will mark the seam in their own story: the line where the machine's version stops being their life.

I still sleep facing the direction I was given. My father says Dharaa also means flowing water, in Sanskrit. Naturally, I checked; he's right.
