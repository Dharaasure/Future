Many Incidents

My parents took my birth details to an astrologer and came home with my name, Dharaa: thread, in Kannada, with a second A for numerology. In our house, astrology was just there, like the plumbing.

I still read my chart for luck, but I believe nothing until I've checked it, so I check its planetary positions in Python against the Swiss Ephemeris. Charts also taught me one habit: read a life across years, never by a single day.

In July, when Doha is too hot for anything but sitting indoors asking questions, I turned my checking on the stories of the Indian migrants I grew up among, my parents' friends and neighbours. I was everyone's daughter and had never asked them anything. So I began an archive. AI is starting to retell lives like theirs, and I wanted to know how far to trust it. I summarized each memory into facts I could check and read it back to the person. Then an AI retold my summary in their voice, and I looked for the seam: the line where the machine runs out of facts and starts inventing.

Subramanya, nineteen years in Qatar, told me about his daughter. She was one when he came here to find work, and his sister promised to look after her in India. In my summary, I had called his sister's promise the one time someone carried him through. "Many times," he said gently. "Many incidents." I crossed out "the one time" and wrote "many incidents" above it. Then I immediately asked him for another single moment. He had just told me his life wasn't made of single moments, and I, who read charts across years, hadn't heard him.

Being wrong usually embarrasses me. This was quieter: he was so kind about it that I almost missed my mistake. That night, when my English-only transcription app turned his Kannada into noise, I didn't want a machine shortening him too. I retyped it myself, three hours at the speed he spoke, Kannada above English, so the words stayed his. Going that slowly, I heard what my summary had flattened: "Of our two eyes," he had said, "one is India, one is Qatar."

The AI worked only from my summaries. Its versions left out thirty-two names and phrases from my recordings. Twenty-four of them, three in every four, were missing from my summaries. The machine had not invented my mistake. It had copied it, then filled the gaps with made-up details, even a line his wife never said.

So I changed how I listen. With Sujith, I asked one question and let it run. For half an hour he talked about the family he supports, the people who helped him leave, and the year he was laid off. Nobody in India knows; they picture a lavish life, and he lets them. My old questions would have found none of it. As a summary, he said, his years here would read as a line. I would have written that line. Now every interview ends with one question: what would a summary miss? And everyone reads their transcript first, free to strike any line.

Checking used to mean stripping a story down to what I could prove. Now it also means checking my version against the person, and assuming my first summary has left someone out. Subramanya could correct me because he was in the room. Most people an AI writes about are not. So in my archive, every AI version sits beside the person's recorded voice, gaps showing. Next, each person will mark the seam in their own story: the line where the machine's version stops being their life.

I had summarized my own name in one word: thread. My father says Dharaa means flowing water, too, in Sanskrit. Naturally, I checked; he's right. Even my name held more than I had written down.
