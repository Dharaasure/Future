Many Incidents

My parents took my birth details to an astrologer and came home with my name. Dharaa means thread in Kannada; the second A is for numerology. Vastu, India's building science, chose the direction I sleep facing. Nobody asked me to believe any of it. It was there, like the plumbing.

I believe nothing until I've checked it, yet I still read my chart for luck. So I check the chart, recomputing its math in Python against the Swiss Ephemeris. Whether the planets mean anything, I haven't settled. But charts taught me the one rule I trust: read a chart whole, across years. Ask it about a single day and it answers confidently, and wrong.

In July, I turned my checking to people: an archive of Indian migrants' memories in Doha. I grew up here as one of their daughters and had never asked them anything. I strip each memory to checkable facts, have an AI retell it in the person's voice, and look for the seam: the line where the machine runs out of facts and starts inventing.

Subramanya, nineteen years in Qatar, gave me the day his daughter was born. My summary said that one day proved the country cared for him. "Many times," he said. I crossed out "the day," wrote "many incidents" above it, then asked my next question about another single day. He had just told me there wasn't one.

Being wrong usually embarrasses me. This was quieter and worse: he had corrected me so gently that I missed it. That night, because my phone's transcriber knows only English, I retyped his Kannada myself at the speed he spoke, three hours, Kannada above English, so the words stayed his. He had called Qatar a second home, then karma bhoomi: the land where your work is done. "Of our two eyes," he said, "one is India, one is Qatar."

The machine made the same mistake I had. Given one fact about his older son, born in India, it moved the birth to Mangalore, a city he is not from, and gave his wife a line she never said. Where it had nothing, it invented; where it had words, it dropped them.

So had I. The AI's versions dropped thirty-two names and phrases from the recordings; twenty-four were already missing from my own summaries.

So I changed how I listen. With Sujith, I asked one question and let it run. Half an hour in, he spoke of the year he was laid off. Nobody in India knows; people there picture a lavish life, and he lets them. As a summary, he said, his years here would read as a line. Now I end every interview by asking what a summary would miss, and that answer never goes to the machine. Val paused before giving her name: someone had once interviewed her, and she never saw what came of it. I made the promise I now make everyone: you read your transcript first, you see the machine's version, and you can strike any line. She went on; later, she said the machine "added a lot of masala."

I had read people the way a chart should never be read, one fact at a time. That works on data. On a person it leaves gaps, and I filled them: Subramanya gave me nineteen years, I wrote down one day, and it felt exactly like checking. He could correct me because he was in the room. Most people a system writes about never are. Next, each person will read the machine's version and mark the seam themselves, where it stops being their life.

I still sleep facing the direction I was given and spell my name with both A's. My father says Dharaa also means flowing water in Sanskrit. I checked the dictionary; he's right. Him, I read the way a chart should be read: whole, across years.
